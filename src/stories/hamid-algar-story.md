## My Life Before Islam

Hamid Algar was born in England on September 21, 1940, and showed from boyhood a remarkable gift for languages, mastering French and German while still at school. He went on to read Arabic and Persian at Trinity College, Cambridge, graduating with first-class honours in 1961. Drawn to the lands he was studying, he made his first journey to Iran in 1959 and travelled widely across the Muslim world as a young student. What began as an academic fascination with Islamic civilisation gradually grew into something far deeper than scholarship, as the living faith he encountered began to reshape his inner life.

## The Path to Islam

Algar continued his studies at the University of Tehran and at Istanbul University, and his years in Iran and Turkey placed him face to face with Islam not merely as a subject of study but as a way of life. He was deeply moved by what he saw: the devotion, the learning, and the dignity of ordinary believers who had made faith the centre of their existence. Over time, his admiration matured into conviction. He recognised in Islam the truth he had been seeking through his years of study, and decided that his knowledge of the faith should be joined by belief in it, a decision that would define the rest of his life.

## The Moment of Guidance

In the early 1960s, during his student years in the Muslim world, Algar embraced Islam, taking the name Taqiuddin Hamid Algar. He first entered Sunni Islam and later chose to follow the Shia school. He has always treated the details of his conversion as a private matter and rarely speaks of them; what matters most, he once remarked with a smile, is that he does not fit the average person's idea of what a Muslim looks like. He returned to Cambridge, completed his doctorate in 1965, and accepted a position at the University of California, Berkeley, where his faith and scholarship would grow together.

## Legacy

Algar taught at Berkeley for forty-five years, from 1965 to 2010, and became one of the world's most distinguished scholars of Islamic studies. He wrote and edited a vast body of work, contributed more than a hundred articles to the Encyclopaedia Iranica, and translated the writings of contemporary thinkers such as Imam Khomeini and Ali Shariati. Beyond the lecture hall, he took part in the life of local mosques and regularly delivered the Friday sermon on the Berkeley campus. For generations of students and colleagues, he exemplified the rare union of rigorous scholarship and sincere faith.
