## My Life Before Islam

Yvonne Ridley was born on 23 April 1958 in Consett, County Durham, in the north of England, and rose to become the chief reporter of the Sunday Express, one of Fleet Street's most respected newspapers. A fearless investigative journalist, she was known for her persistence, her sharp questions, and a life that had little room for religion. By her own account she was far from any faith, and Islam, like many in the West, she knew mostly through headlines. Nothing in her career had prepared her for what would happen to her in September 2001, an ordeal that would place her name before the eyes of the entire world.

## The Path to Islam

On 28 September 2001, days after the September 11 attacks, Ridley crossed into Afghanistan, disguised as an Afghan woman in a burka, to report on the country under Taliban rule. She was captured and held for eleven days. To her surprise she was treated with courtesy throughout her captivity, and she was deeply impressed by the generosity of her captors, who did not fit the violent image she had been conditioned to expect. As she later explained, she promised the man who questioned her that she would read the Qur'an after her release, and on returning home she honored that promise, a decision that quietly changed her life.

## The Moment of Guidance

Reading the Qur'an turned Yvonne Ridley's world upside down. What began as a promise fulfilled in passing became a genuine search for truth, and in June 2003 she embraced Islam, saying that it gave her "the biggest and the best family in the world." She has acknowledged that the courtesy shown to her during her captivity played an important part in her conversion, and that she found in Islam not a sentimental attachment but a complete way of life. When an interviewer accused her of converting for publicity, she replied that her conversion had cost her career, not advanced it, a measure of the sincerity of her faith.

## Legacy

After her conversion Ridley became a prominent Muslim voice in Britain, chairing the Respect Party and later working as a journalist for Press TV, where she reported from the Muslim world and championed the rights of the Palestinian people. She has written and spoken widely about Islam, defending the religion against the prejudices she once shared and inviting the West to a more generous understanding of it. Her journey, from a skeptical Fleet Street reporter to a captive of the Taliban, to a believing Muslim, is one of the most extraordinary conversions of modern times, a reminder that guidance can reach a heart through the most unexpected doors.
