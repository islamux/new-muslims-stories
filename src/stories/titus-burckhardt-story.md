## My Life Before Islam

Titus Burckhardt was born on 24 October 1908 in Florence, into one of the most distinguished families of Basel; his father was the sculptor Carl Burckhardt, his great-uncle the great historian Jacob Burckhardt, and his family tree included John Lewis Burckhardt, the celebrated explorer of Petra and Abu Simbel. Raised a Protestant in Switzerland, he studied painting, sculpture, and art history in Munich and Paris, intending to become a sculptor like his father. Yet the modern West, with its restless secularism, left him unsatisfied. Drawn to the beauty and permanence of traditional civilizations, he found himself looking eastward, toward a world in which art, faith, and daily life were still one.

## The Path to Islam

In his late teens Burckhardt visited Morocco, where the traditional Muslim world captivated him, and he devoted himself to drawing and painting its people and cities. Back in Europe he discovered the writings of the French metaphysician René Guénon, in whom, in the words of his biographer, "he found the key to the world that had entranced him." Convinced that Islam must be understood from within, he returned to Morocco in 1933 in search of a spiritual master and settled in Fez, the ancient city of learning, where he was received into the Darqawiyya Sufi order by Sheikh Ali al-Darqawi.

## The Moment of Guidance

It was in Fez that Titus Burckhardt embraced Islam and took the name Ibrahim Izz al-Din, beginning an extraordinary immersion in the Islamic sciences. He learned Arabic well enough to read the Sufi classics in their original language, memorized the Alfiyya of Ibn Malik under a master craftsman, and even worked for a time as a shepherd in the Middle Atlas to earn his keep. He translated the writings of great masters such as Ibn Arabi and the letters of his own shaykh, and he assimilated the metaphysics of Sufism not as an academic observer but as a believer who had submitted his whole being to its truth.

## Legacy

Burckhardt returned to Switzerland and devoted his life to expounding the wisdom of Islam and of other sacred traditions through a remarkable body of work. His books "Introduction to Sufi Doctrine," "Sacred Art in East and West," "Fez, City of Islam," and "Art of Islam: Language and Meaning" remain landmarks, and he is counted among the great voices of the perennialist school alongside Schuon and Guénon. As artistic director of the Urs Graf publishing house he produced exquisite facsimiles of medieval manuscripts, and his enduring love of Fez helped draw the world's attention to the threatened beauty of the old city. He died in Lausanne in 1984, leaving writings of rare depth and light.
