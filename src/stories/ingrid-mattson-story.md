## My Life Before Islam

Ingrid Mattson was born on August 24, 1963, in Kingston, Ontario, the sixth of seven children in a Christian family. Her father was a criminal lawyer and her mother raised the children at home. She attended Catholic schools, and she later credited the Catholic women who taught her with giving her both a fine education and a place to explore an early, youthful spirituality. By sixteen, however, she had stopped attending church: she realised that she did not believe what she was being taught, and she left religion behind. At the University of Waterloo she studied philosophy and fine arts, embracing existentialism and its insistence that meaning is made through the choices a person makes.

## The Path to Islam

In the summer of 1986 she went to Paris as a visiting student, and there she befriended West African students from a Sufi Muslim community. She was struck by their faith and by the way they carried it. When she read the Qur'an for herself, she found what she described as an awareness of God for the first time since she was very young. The book did not ask her to abandon reason; it asked her to take responsibility for her choices before the One who had made her. She returned to Waterloo and, in 1987, converted to Islam, completing her degrees in philosophy and fine arts the same year.

## The Moment of Guidance

After graduating she went to Pakistan, where in 1989 she worked in a crowded Afghan refugee camp near Peshawar, teaching girls and trying to improve conditions for their families. It was there that she met Amer Aetak, an Egyptian engineer digging wells and building housing, and the two were quietly married. She also encountered the Taliban before they came to power: in one small area of the camp they refused to let her teach girls. That experience shaped her lifelong opposition to religious extremism and her conviction that education, especially for women, is among the first things extremists try to take away.

## Legacy

She went on to earn a PhD in Near Eastern Languages and Civilizations from the University of Chicago in 1999, specialising in Islamic law and society. From 1998 to 2012 she taught Islamic studies and Christian-Muslim relations at Hartford Seminary, where she founded the first accredited graduate programme for Muslim chaplains in America. In 2001 she became the first woman to serve as vice-president of the Islamic Society of North America, and in 2006 the first woman to serve as its president. She later became the London and Windsor Community Chair in Islamic Studies at Huron University College in London, Ontario. She also founded the Hurma Project to confront spiritual and sexual abuse in Muslim communities, and in 2026 received an honorary doctorate from Yale. Her book, *The Story of the Qur'an*, has introduced the scripture to a generation of readers.
