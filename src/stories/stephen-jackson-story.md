## My Life Before Islam

Stephen Jackson was born on 5 April 1978 in Houston, Texas, and grew up in Port Arthur, where basketball became his path out of a difficult environment. After one season of college basketball he entered the NBA draft in 1997 and, though chosen late, built a long and respected fourteen-year career with teams including the San Antonio Spurs, the Golden State Warriors, and the Indiana Pacers. He won an NBA championship with the Spurs in 2003 and became a beloved figure in the Warriors' celebrated "We Believe" playoff run. Yet by his own account, a life shaped by struggle and fame left him searching for something deeper than the achievements and noise of professional sport.

## The Path to Islam

Jackson had known about Islam since his high-school years, when a friend's family showed him a religion of structure and balance. "I saw the structure and balance of this religion," he recalled, "and I knew there was something special there." During his career he counted Muslim friends among his closest companions, and he has said he long tried to live as a Muslim man in conduct and values. The murder of his friend Jibreel, with whom he had prayed, and the striking calm of Jibreel's mother at the funeral, marked the day he truly decided; the killing of his close friend George Floyd, whom he called his "twin," later made his own mortality and purpose urgent.

## The Moment of Guidance

On 6 January 2021, Stephen Jackson took his shahada in a small gathering led by his friend, the rapper Tone Trump. "I came to Islam for Allah and Allah alone," he said. "My journey to Islam came because of my heart." He has described himself as endlessly inspired by Malcolm X, whom he calls the most respected American Muslim ever, and he says he prays five times a day: "Everything I have today is because I get on my knees and pray." At forty-two, guided by grief, discipline, and a long-loved faith, he stepped firmly from the edge of certainty into a new life.

## Legacy

Since his conversion, Jackson has spoken openly about Islam on his popular podcast and in interviews, encouraging other athletes to study the faith and answering questions without embarrassment. In a world where famous Muslims are often private about their religion, his openness has made him a source of pride, especially for young African-American Muslims who see a man they admire kneeling in prayer and standing up for justice. Combining his activism for civil rights with his new faith, he carries on the long American tradition of Islam as a religion of the oppressed and the believing. His story is one of finding peace, purpose, and a fuller humanity at last.
