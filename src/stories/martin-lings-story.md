## My Life Before Islam

Martin Lings was born in Burnage, Manchester, on 24 January 1909, into a Protestant family. He was raised a Christian but became an atheist, and he travelled widely from an early age, spending significant time in the United States because of his father's work. He was educated at Clifton College and then at Magdalen College, Oxford, where he took a degree in English language and literature and became first a student and then a close friend of C. S. Lewis. After Oxford he taught Anglo-Saxon and Middle English at the Vytautas Magnus University in Lithuania.

## The Path to Islam

At Oxford, Lings had discovered the writings of the French metaphysician René Guénon, a Muslim convert, and of the Swiss spiritual teacher Frithjof Schuon. Their vision of a perennial wisdom underlying the world's religions answered something in him that neither atheism nor the Christianity of his upbringing had settled. He followed the trail to its source: in 1938 he travelled to Basel to meet Schuon, and there he embraced the branch of the Alawiyya Sufi order that Schuon led. He remained Schuon's disciple for the rest of his life.

## The Moment of Guidance

The following year, 1939, Lings went to Cairo to visit a friend who was an assistant of Guénon. Soon after he arrived, the friend died, and Lings — now in a Muslim country, at the beginning of a war — began to study Arabic. Cairo became his home for more than a decade: he taught English at the University of Cairo and produced a Shakespeare play every year. In 1944 he married Lesley Smalley, and they lived in a village near the pyramids. In 1952 anti-British disturbances forced him to leave Egypt, but he left as a Muslim scholar, not a visitor.

## Legacy

Back in Britain, Lings took a degree in Arabic and then a doctorate at the School of Oriental and African Studies; his thesis became a book on the Algerian Sufi Ahmad al-Alawi. He worked at the British Museum and then the British Library, rising to Keeper of Oriental Printed Books and Manuscripts from 1970 to 1973. But his most famous work came later: Muhammad: His Life Based on the Earliest Sources, published in 1983, which won prizes from the governments of Pakistan and Egypt and was acclaimed at the National Seerat Conference in Islamabad as the best biography of the Prophet in English. Known in the Muslim world as Abu Bakr Siraj ad-Din, he died in Kent on 12 May 2005, leaving a body of work that made the classical Islamic tradition legible to English readers.
